What to Look for When Hiring a Commercial Cleaner

S
Sunrise Specialty Partners
Bay Area Commercial Cleaning Experts
Published: November 11, 2026
Last updated: November 11, 2026
8 min read
Key Takeaway

Before you hire a commercial cleaner in California, check five things: a current janitorial registration with the state Labor Commissioner, general liability insurance, workers' compensation, workers who are employees, and a written scope of work. Price is the last thing to compare, not the first.

Why Most Offices Hire Wrong (And Pay for It Later)

Most offices pick a cleaning company the same way: ask two or three for a price, take the lowest, and sign. The problems arrive later. Visits get missed. The crew changes every month. Nobody can say what was actually included.

In California there is a second problem that most office managers have never heard of. If you hire a janitorial company that is not registered with the state, you can be fined. The law puts the fine on the customer at $2,000 to $10,000 for a first violation and $10,000 to $25,000 after that.

Staffing is the other thing price hides. The Bureau of Labor Statistics projects about 321,800 openings for janitors and building cleaners each year, most of them to replace people who leave the job. A company that cannot keep its people cannot keep your office consistent, however good the first month looks.

The Non-Negotiables: What to Verify Before You Sign

These are baseline checks, not extras. Every one of them can be confirmed with a document or a lookup, so there is no reason to take anyone's word for it.

1. A Current California Janitorial Registration

Since July 1, 2018, every janitorial employer in California must register with the Labor Commissioner and renew each year. You do not register as the customer, but you are the one fined if your cleaner is not. Ask for the registration number, then check it yourself in the state's free registration search. It takes about a minute.

2. General Liability Insurance

This covers damage to your office and injuries to other people caused by the cleaner's work. A common requirement is $1 million per occurrence and $2 million in aggregate. Those are the limits the State of California asks of its own contractors. Ask for a certificate of insurance and ask to be named as an additional insured. Then ask for the endorsement itself. The same state guidance says a note on the certificate is not acceptable in place of the actual endorsement.

3. Workers' Compensation Coverage

All California employers must carry workers' compensation for their employees. Operating without it is a criminal offense, and state penalties can reach $100,000. Ask for the certificate. You can also look a company up in the Workers' Compensation Insurance Rating Bureau's free coverage inquiry tool.

4. Cleaners Who Are Employees

California starts from the assumption that every worker is an employee. To treat someone as an independent contractor, a company has to prove three things, including that the work falls outside the usual course of its business. Cleaning is a cleaning company's usual business, so that is hard to prove. Willful misclassification carries penalties of $5,000 to $25,000 per violation.

It matters to you because contractors paid on a 1099 are not covered by the company's workers' compensation, and because a business that hires a janitorial company can, in some circumstances, be held jointly liable for that company's unpaid wages under California Labor Code section 238.5.

5. Background Checks Done Lawfully

Night crews work in your office when nobody else is there, so it is fair to ask how staff are screened. Listen to how the answer is phrased. California's Fair Chance Act generally bars employers with five or more employees from asking about conviction history before making a job offer, and San Francisco's Fair Chance Ordinance adds limits on what can be considered at all. A good answer describes checks made after a conditional offer, with each case reviewed individually. "We never hire anyone with a record" is not a good answer. It describes a process that breaks the law.

6. Verifiable Commercial References

Cleaning homes and cleaning offices are different jobs. Ask for two or three current office clients close to your size, then call them. Ask how long they have used the company, whether the same crew still shows up, and how the last problem was handled.

7. A Written Scope of Work

"We clean everything" is how disputes start. The scope should list each task, how often it is done, who supplies paper goods and soap, and what counts as extra. Read the renewal and cancellation terms as well. California's automatic renewal law protects consumers, not businesses, so whatever your contract says about auto-renewal and notice periods is what applies.

The Right Questions to Ask During the Vetting Call

Treat the first call as an interview. Good companies answer these quickly and send documents the same day. How a company responds tells you as much as what it says.

  • "Can you send your registration number and certificates of insurance today?" A company that has them sends them. A company that stalls usually has a reason.
  • "Are the people cleaning my office your employees?" You want a plain yes, with payroll, not a network of subcontractors nobody has met.
  • "Will the same crew come each visit, and what happens when someone is out?" Consistency is most of what you are paying for.
  • "Who supervises the crew, and how do I reach that person?" You should get a name and a phone number, not a general inbox.
  • "Which products do you use, and can I see the safety data sheets?" Federal rules require employers to keep safety data sheets for the chemicals their workers use. Look for products carrying the EPA's Safer Choice label.
  • "How much notice do I give to cancel, and does the contract renew on its own?" Get the answer in writing before you sign.

Red Flags to Watch For

Any one of these is a reason to slow down. Two or more is a reason to move on.

  • No registration number, or one that does not appear in the state search. This is the one that can cost you a fine directly.
  • Insurance certificates that are "on the way." If coverage exists, the certificate takes minutes to send.
  • A price with no walkthrough. Nobody can price your office accurately without seeing it.
  • A price far below every other bid. California Labor Code section 2810 says a business must not sign a janitorial contract when it knows, or should know, that the price is too low for the contractor to comply with the law.
  • Crews paid in cash or as 1099 contractors. See point 4 above.
  • A scope that fits in one sentence. If it is not written down, it is not included.
