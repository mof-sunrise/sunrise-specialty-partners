The Real Cost of a Dirty Office (And How to Fix It)

S
Sunrise Specialty Partners
Bay Area Commercial Cleaning Experts
Published: November 11, 2026
Last updated: November 11, 2026
8 min read
Key Takeaway

No one can put a single reliable dollar figure on a dirty office, and the big numbers quoted online do not hold up. What the research does support is narrower and still worth acting on: germs cross an office within hours, people in cleaner offices rate their own productivity higher, and visitors judge a business by its restrooms and floors.

What 'Clean Enough' Is Costing You

Search for the cost of a dirty office and you will find the same figure everywhere: illness costs US employers $225.8 billion a year, or $1,685 per employee. It is a real number, repeated by the CDC Foundation in 2015. But it comes from a 2003 phone survey, it covers every health condition from back pain to depression, and 71 percent of it is people working below par, not people staying home. It says nothing about cleaning.

We would rather give you numbers you can defend. Start with one that is simple and local: what a lost day of work costs.

$386
One day of average wages in the San Francisco metro area The mean hourly wage across all occupations here was $48.19 in May 2025, against $33.54 nationally. Eight hours at that rate is about $386, before benefits. Source: U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics, San Francisco-Oakland-Fremont, May 2025

Every day someone on your team is out sick, or in the office but running at half speed, costs something close to that. The question for the rest of this article is how much of it a cleaner office can prevent. The evidence points to three places it can help.

The Productivity Hit: What Clutter and Grime Do to Focus

You will often read that "a Princeton study proved clutter hurts productivity." It did not. The 2011 study scanned people's brains while they looked at shapes on a screen. It found that several things in view compete for the brain's attention, and that attention has to work harder when they do. That fits the idea that a messy space is tiring. It is not a study of desks or offices.

The closest thing to direct evidence is a 2018 study of five Dutch organizations. Researchers measured how clean each office really was, including dust levels, then surveyed the people working there. Staff in the cleaner offices rated their own productivity and job satisfaction higher. It is a small study and people scored themselves, so treat it as a signal and not a proof.

Air is the better-documented route. Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory's review of the research describes lab studies where removing an old, dirty carpet improved typing, arithmetic and proofreading by about 4 percent. And in a Harvard study, 24 office workers scored 61 percent higher on decision-making tests on days when the air held fewer chemical vapors. Cleaning products are one source of those vapors, which is a reason to ask what your cleaner uses, not only how often they come.

Sick Days and the Hidden Cost of Poor Hygiene

This is where the evidence is strongest about the problem and most modest about the cure.

"Within 2 to 4 hours between 40 to 60% of the fomites sampled were contaminated with virus."
Charles Gerba, University of Arizona, American Society for Microbiology, 2014. Fomites are surfaces and objects that carry germs.

Gerba's team had placed a harmless tracer virus on a single doorknob or tabletop in an office building. By lunchtime it was on about half the surfaces they tested. Other studies tell you which surfaces to worry about:

One local detail from the first study: San Francisco offices were less contaminated than those in New York or Tucson. Several of these studies were funded in part by makers of cleaning and hand hygiene products, which is worth knowing when you read them.

Does cleaning these surfaces keep people at work? The honest answer is "partly." In the 2019 study, a hygiene program cut the tracer virus on surfaces by 85 percent. In a year-long German trial, office staff given hand sanitizer had fewer days with cold symptoms, but the drop in days absent was not statistically significant. In a trial of 1,386 US office workers, health insurance claims for preventable illnesses fell 20.9 percent, and again the change in absences was too small to be sure of.

So the fair claim is this: good hygiene clearly reduces what is on your surfaces, and probably reduces how often people feel ill. Nobody has shown that it sharply cuts sick days. For scale, about 2.2 percent of full-time US workers are absent for illness or injury in a typical week.

First Impressions: What Clients See That You've Stopped Noticing

Work in a space every day and you stop seeing it. The stain by the door, the dust on the reception shelf and the tired restroom fade into the background. A client or a job candidate walking in for the first time sees all of it at once.

And people do judge. In a 2021 Harris Poll of more than 2,000 US adults, 74 percent said dirty restrooms hurt their view of a business, 68 percent said the same of dirty floors, and 57 percent of dirty entryways. The survey was paid for by Cintas, which sells restroom supplies, so read it with that in mind. The ranking is still useful: restrooms first, floors second, entrance third. If you can only raise the standard in three places, raise it there.

A simple test is to walk in through the front door as a visitor would, once a month, and write down the first three things you notice.

Calculating Your ROI on Professional Cleaning

You can run the numbers for your own office in two minutes.

  • Cost of one lost workday: about $386 in wages at the Bay Area average. Use your own team's figure if you have it. Nationally, employers spend $46.89 per hour worked once benefits are included.
  • Cost of cleaning: your monthly quote.
  • Break-even: monthly quote divided by the cost of a lost day. That is how many lost days the cleaning would need to prevent each month to pay for itself on sick days alone.

As an example, take a 20-person office with a cleaning quote of $1,500 a month. $1,500 divided by $386 is about 4 lost days a month, across the whole team. Given the research above, cleaning is unlikely to prevent that many on its own.

That is the honest conclusion: cleaning rarely pays for itself through sick days alone. The case rests on all three effects together. People feel ill less often. They think better of where they work. Visitors think better of you. Cleaning is a basic running cost, like power and internet. The real return comes from buying the right level of it at a fair price, which is the reason to compare several written proposals against the same scope before you sign.
