How Often Should Your Office Really Be Cleaned?

S
Sunrise Specialty Partners
Bay Area Commercial Cleaning Experts
Published: November 11, 2026
Last updated: November 11, 2026
7 min read
Key Takeaway

No law tells an office how many times a week to clean. The rules only say it must be kept clean and sanitary. A sound starting point: restrooms, kitchens, trash and high-touch surfaces every day the office is in use, floors two or three times a week, detail work monthly, and a deep clean once or twice a year. Then adjust for how many people actually come in.

Why Cleaning Frequency Matters More Than You Think

Ask three cleaning companies how often your office needs cleaning and you will get three answers. That is because there is no official number. Federal workplace rules say only that places of employment must be kept clean to the extent the nature of the work allows. California's own sanitation rule says much the same. The one fixed interval in the federal standard is for food waste: those bins must be emptied at least once each working day.

So frequency is a judgment call, and it helps to know what the judgment rests on. The main reason to clean on a schedule, and not just when things look dirty, is that what people carry on their hands moves through a building far faster than dirt builds up.

How fast a virus spread through an office building Researchers placed a harmless tracer virus on one doorknob or tabletop. Within 2 to 4 hours it was on 40 to 60 percent of the surfaces they sampled. Source: Charles Gerba, University of Arizona, presented to the American Society for Microbiology, 2014 (conference presentation)

In the same study, wiping surfaces daily with disinfecting wipes and practicing hand hygiene cut the number of contaminated surfaces by more than 80 percent. That is the case for a routine. A weekly clean cannot keep up with something that crosses the office before lunch.

The Standard Cleaning Schedule: A Room-by-Room Breakdown

Different parts of an office get dirty at different speeds, so one frequency for the whole space is always wrong somewhere. The schedule below is our recommended starting point. Where a public standard exists, we have linked it. Where none exists, it is practical judgment.

Daily: High-Touch Surfaces and Common Areas

On every day the office is in use: restrooms cleaned and restocked, kitchen counters and sinks wiped, trash and food waste removed, and high-touch surfaces wiped down. The CDC's guidance is to clean high-touch surfaces regularly and other surfaces when visibly dirty, and it lists door handles, stair rails, elevator buttons, restroom fixtures and desks among them. Entrances and corridors with carpet should be vacuumed daily too. The Carpet and Rug Institute's commercial guidelines call for daily vacuuming in heavy-traffic areas such as entrances, corridors and break areas.

Two or Three Times a Week: Floors in Working Areas

Private offices and meeting rooms see less traffic. The same guidelines say these can be vacuumed two or three times a week, with spots checked and treated daily. Hard floors in working areas can follow the same rhythm. Vacuuming does most of the work: the institute says 79 percent or more of soil in carpet can be removed dry.

Weekly: Glass, Appliances and a Fuller Kitchen Clean

Once a week, clean glass partitions and entry doors, wipe down the inside of the microwave and the outside of the refrigerator, dust desks and shelves, and do a more thorough clean of the kitchen and restrooms than the daily pass allows.

Monthly: Detail Work and Overlooked Areas

Baseboards, vents and air returns, light fixtures, the tops of cabinets, upholstered chairs, and the floor under desks. None of this shows on a normal day. All of it shows after three months of skipping it.

Once or Twice a Year: The Deep Clean

Carpet extraction, interior window washing, and floor refinishing where needed. The Carpet and Rug Institute sets no fixed interval for deep carpet cleaning. It depends on traffic. Many offices settle on once or twice a year. Air systems are a separate job: the duct cleaning industry's standard recommends inspecting commercial air handling units and ducts at least once a year.

Adjusting for Your Office Size and Headcount

Square footage tells you how long a clean takes. Headcount tells you how often you need one. A 5-person firm in 3,000 square feet needs less than a 40-person team in the same space. These are starting points, not standards:

Office SizeHeadcountA Reasonable Starting Point
Under 2,000 sq ft1–15 people1 to 2 visits a week, plus a daily wipe of the kitchen and door handles by staff
2,000–5,000 sq ft15–50 people3 visits a week, timed to your busiest days
5,000–15,000 sq ft50–150 people5 nights a week
15,000+ sq ft150+ people5 nights a week, plus daytime restroom and kitchen checks

Some workplaces have rules of their own. Medical and dental offices, food businesses and childcare centers answer to their own regulators. If you are in one of those fields, start with that regulator's requirements and build the schedule around them.

Signs Your Office Isn't Being Cleaned Often Enough

You do not need an inspection to tell. Look for dust on screens and shelves between visits, trash bins that smell by the afternoon, restrooms that run out of soap or paper, sticky kitchen counters in the morning, and carpet with visible paths worn into it. If people have started cleaning their own desks before they sit down, they have already noticed.

Facilities managers use five levels of cleanliness published by APPA, the association for educational facilities, to put a name to this. Level 1 is "Orderly Spotlessness." Level 2 is "Ordinary Tidiness," which is where most offices aim. Level 3 is "Casual Inattention," where dust and smudges are obvious. If your office reads as Level 3 the day before the cleaners come, the gap between visits is too long.

"In most situations, cleaning regularly is enough to prevent the spread of germs."
U.S. Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, When and How to Clean and Disinfect a Facility

That line matters when someone offers you daily disinfection of everything. The CDC's advice is to disinfect where people have obviously been ill, and to consider it for high-traffic areas. For the rest of the office, regular cleaning does the job.

How Bay Area Offices Compare to National Standards

The biggest local factor is that offices here are emptier than most, and unevenly so. In the week of October 27, 2026, Kastle's badge-swipe data put San Francisco offices at 48.1 percent of their pre-pandemic occupancy on average, against 56.5 percent across ten large US cities. On the Tuesday of that week San Francisco reached 59.9 percent, its highest peak of the year.

That gap between the average and the peak is the point. An office that is busy Tuesday through Thursday and quiet on Monday and Friday does not need the same clean five nights a week. It needs full cleans after the busy days and a lighter pass, or none, after the quiet ones. Traffic is climbing, though. Placer.ai's office index showed San Francisco office visits in September 2026 up 12.2 percent on a year earlier, the strongest growth of any city it tracks, so a schedule set in 2024 is worth revisiting.

Two California points are also worth knowing. During wildfire smoke, Cal/OSHA's guidance for indoor workplaces recommends checking that building air filters are not dirty, damaged or leaking, and using the highest-rated filter the system can take. Ask your building manager about this before smoke season, not during it. And any janitorial company you hire must be registered with the California Labor Commissioner, whatever schedule you choose.
